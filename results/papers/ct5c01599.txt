ABSTRACT: Coupled-cluster theory (CC) is a highly accurate and versatile
method for simulating complex interactions within quantum systems. The
extension of CC theory to model mixed electron-photon processes with quantum
electrodynamics (QED) has improved our capability to predict cavity-modified
chemistry, a field where photons are used as cost-effective and eco-friendly
alternatives to catalyze/inhibit chemical reactions. However, calculations with CC
methods, even without incorporating QED effects, are often prohibitively
expensive. Simulations of larger systems require scalable infrastructures that exist
for traditional CC methods but not for QED-CC methods. As such, we present a
GPU-enabled, high-performance, open-source implementation of the quantum
electrodynamics coupled-cluster method with single and double excitations (QED-CCSD) within the ExaChem quantum chemistry
software package. ExaChem relies on the Tensor Algebra for Many-body Methods (TAMM) infrastructure: a parallel heterogeneous
tensorlibrarydesigned to achieve scalable performanceon modern heterogeneous supercomputingplatforms. We discuss theoretical
foundations, algorithmic details, and numerical benchmarks to showcase the larger systems that ExaChem can simulate and how the
integration of photonic degrees-of-freedom alters their ground-state properties.
■INTRODUCTION
The growing need for accurate quantum mechanical
descriptions of quantum-driven technologies, such as quantum
computing and quantum sensing, has advanced methodologies
within computational chemistry and quantum physics.1−4
Advancements in high-performance computing now enable
large-scale applications of these highly accurate techniques
beyond model chemical systems.5,6
Recent interest in applying computational chemistry
techniques to polariton chemistry has gained traction as its
ability to modify chemical structures, properties, and reactivity
solelythroughphotonicinteractionswithmolecular,electronic,
vibrational, and rovibrational states is explored.7−14 Although
several research groups within the quantum optics community
have developed model Hamiltonians to infer qualitative
features of polaritonic physics,15−20 a holistic quantitative
theoretical understanding of molecular polaritons demands the
equal treatment of electronic and photonic degrees-of-
freedom; this is achieved with ab initio quantum electro-
dynamics (QED) methodologies. QED methodologies for-
mulate electron-photon interactions within molecules from
first-principles such that photon coupling is characterized for
the entire electronic structure rather than for only a select few
states.
Available quantum electrodynamics extensions of various
electronic structure methods include: density functional theory
(QED-DFT21−30) with real-time22,23,31−34 and linear-response
formulations28,35,36 of QED time-dependent density functional
theory (QED-TDDFT); the mean-field QED Hartree−Fock
(QED-HF) method37 and strong-coupling QED-HF (SC-
QED-HF) method;38 the configuration interaction (QED-CI),
full configuration interaction,39,40 and the more recently
complete active space configuration interaction (QED-
CASCI)41 methods;second-orderMøller−Plessetperturbation
theory (QED-MP2) and the fully origin-independent strong-
coupling QED-MP242,43 for charged systems. Other cavity
QED extensions include algebraic diagrammatic construction
(QED-ADC44), variational QED-2-RDM methods,45 diffusion
Monte Carlo,46 density matrix renormalization group (QED-
DMRG),47 and QED-complete active space self-consistent
field (QED-CASSCF).48,49
Alongside the aforementioned approaches, the time-
independent coupled-cluster (CC) method, with its numerous
extensions,50−59 serves as a benchmark for many electronic
structure methods. The broad appeal and success of CC

for the correlated ground-state wave function |Ψ⟩: |Ψ⟩ = eT

reference wave function, typically�but not exclusively�a

Two generalizations of coupled-cluster (CC) theory for
incorporating quantum electrodynamics (QED) effects
emerged around the same time. Mordovina et al.60 introduced
the polaritonic coupled-cluster theory, which used an
exponential parametrization of the ground-state polaritonic
wave function. This parametrization included single and
double electronic transition operators, photon creation
operators, and combined electron transition and photon
creation operators. They applied this method, along with
QED full configuration interaction, to study strong coupling
between a single photon mode and a four-site Hubbard model.
Notably, they used nilpotent operators instead of conventional
boson creation operators, resulting in a linear parametrization
of the photon space. In contrast, the QED-CCSD-1 model by
Haugland et al.37 adopted a similarly complex exponential
parametrization but used conventional (non-nilpotent) boson
creation operators. They applied this model to address strong
coupling in an ab initio molecular Hamiltonian.
Several research teams have developed and applied similar
QED-CC approaches to study the impact of cavity effects on
various ground-state properties. DePrince61 used QED-CCSD-
1 to show how strong coupling alters electron affinities in
sodiumhalidecompounds significantly.However, cavityeffects
had less influence on ionization potentials in these systems.
Pavošević and Flick62 further investigated the effects of
cavity interactions on electron affinities by employing a unitary
formulation of QED-CCSD-1. They used the variational
quantum eigensolver (VQE) algorithm63 on a quantum
computer for this purpose. Additionally, they expanded the
framework to include up to two-photon creation operators,
alongside single and double electronic excitations, which they
called QED-CCSD-2.
These foundational studies paved the way for further
exploration of ionization characteristics in QED environments
byRisoetal.64 underliningthecriticalimportanceofaccurately
treating the ionized electron.
Recent studies utilizing QED-CC methodologies have
explored the influence of vacuum fluctuations in chemical
systems, focusing on ground-state modifications in cavity-
embedded systems rather than transitions or polariton
generationvia introduced photons. Pavoševićet al.65 employed
the nonunitary QED-CCSD-2 approach, showing that strong
coupling can significantly alter proton transfer reaction barrier
heights. They also proposed an approximation, QED-CCSD-
21, which pairs single-electron transitions with up to two-
photon creation operators, and double-electron transitions
with single-photon creation operators, akin to the electron−
phonon interaction model of White et al.66 Other studies on
quantum vacuum effects include catalytic control of Diels−
Alder reactions,67 cavity-catalyzed azide−alkyne cycloaddi-
tion,68 and the impact on intermolecular forces like van der
Waals interactions.69,70
Pavošević and Rubio29 incorporated QED-CCSD-1 into an
embedding protocol. This approach focuses on a portion of a
cavity-embedded molecular system using QED-CC, while the
remaining part of the system is analyzed using either QED-
DFT or QED-HF, termed as “QED-CC-in-QED-SCF”.
Assuming that electron-photon correlations are localized
within the embedded region, this novel protocol could
potentially alleviate the computational complexities inherent
in the many-body ab initio cavity QED framework. For
broader discussions on ab initio methods for polariton
chemistry, we refer the reader to comprehensive reviews (see
refs 71−74).
This work focuses on the implementation of the QED-CC
method using the Pauli−Fierz Hamiltonian, employing a
coherent state basis to account for single, double, and mixed
excitations within both electronic and bosonic degrees of
freedom. The method is developed within ExaChem,75 a
parallel heterogeneous computing platform built atop the
Tensor Algebra for Many-body Methods (TAMM),76 a
distributed tensor library optimized for parallel execution on
modern heterogeneous architectures. This implementation of
QED-CCSD within ExaChem enables simulations of larger
and more complex systems than before, while accurately
capturing the intricate interplay between electronic and
bosonic degrees of freedom. In the sections that follow, we
describe the theoretical foundations of the QED-CC method-
ology, outline its implementation within the open-source
ExaChem platform, present the computational performance
and simulation results, and conclude with a discussion of key
findings and future directions.

The CCSD(2, 0) involves performing electronic CCSD
calculations with the QED Fock matrix elements and two-
electron integrals that are transformed using the coefficient
matrix elements obtained from the QED-HF procedure. We
can also obtain the same results as CCSD(2, 0) by setting ωcav
= 0 in either CCSD(2, 1) or CCSD(2, 2) calculations. The
parameter ωcav is inversely proportional to the length of the
cavity, L (ωcav ∝ 1/L), meaning it is essentially performing
QED-CCSD(m, n) simulations with a cavity of infinite length.
The CCSD(2, 0) approach does not require any new
amplitude tensor definitions compared to the canonical CCSD
method; the electronic T1 and T2 tensors, also denoted as T(1,
0) and T(2, 0), are sufficient. In contrast, the CCSD(2, 1)
scheme requires a total of five kinds of amplitude tensors,
namely T(1, 0), T(2, 0), T(0, 1), T(1, 1), and T(2, 1). The
CCSD(2, 2) requires an additional three new tensors T(0, 2),
T(1, 2), and T(2, 2). Out of these, T(0, 1) and T(0, 2) are
rank-zero tensors or scalars. Since the bosonic part is a scalar,
the mixedtensors T(1,1), T(2,1), T(1,2), and T(2,2)do not
increase any dimensionality in the tensor definition. Therefore,

TAMM provides a high-level tensor algebra interface that
allows users to define distributed block-sparse tensors and
express tensor operations over them in familiar mathematical
notation. Examples of tensor expressions used in the
implementation of QED-CC methods can be seen in Figure
1. The framework provides a flexible infrastructure for
describing tensor objects using index spaces and tiling
capabilities, allowing users to define tiling over index spaces
to construct tiled index spaces that represent blocked tensors.
This enables efficient data distribution and tensor operations
that better leverage the underlying hardware. Tensor
operations are managed by a scheduler backend that employs
data flow analysis over queued operations to find dependencies
and construct load-balanced, communication-efficient sched-
ules for execution. TAMM achieves scalability through the use
of Global Arrays86 and MPI for distributed memory
parallelism, while leveraging optimized vendor libraries to
ensure high-performance execution on CPUs and accelerators.
The second major component in the ExaChem implemen-
tation is the p†q symbolic algebra library.87 This library
automates the derivation ofmany-body expressions with mixed
Fermionic and bosonic second-quantized operators. The initial
QED-CCSD(2,2) expressions are fed into p†q and sub-
sequently brought to normal order, while identifying and
merging permutations into a fully contracted programmable
representation. An example python script to generate the
doubles + two-photon spin−orbital residuals, with generated
output, is given in Figure 1. After generation of the residual
equations, the QED-CCSD expressions are further expanded
into spin-explicit tensors that have smaller dimensionality and
thus a smaller memory footprint.

codevia thePQ-Graphengine. The expressionsareinterpreted
as a directed acyclic graph, where the tensors are the vertices
and the edges determine the contraction. The graph
representation enables symbolic optimization techniques such
as contraction reordering and the identification, substitution,
and fusion of common subexpressions. The expressions from

using NumPy, in Psi488 using TiledArray,89 and now in

minimizes the number of floating-point operations and the

■RESULTSANDDISCUSSION
The high-performance implementation of QED-CC methods
in ExaChem enables us to explore QED effects on systems that
were previously computationally prohibitively expensive,
including those with larger numbers of atoms and simulations
with larger basis sets. To demonstrate these new capabilities,
we carried out calculations for three intramolecular proton
transfer reactions in malonaldehyde, aminopropenal, and
porphyrin. A previous study65 examined the proton transfer
reactions in malonaldehyde and aminopropenal using the cc-
pVDZ basis set. In contrast, this work investigates the role of
the larger aug-cc-pVDZ basis set utilizing the new
implementation of QED-CC methods. As a paramount
demonstration of the performance of the newly implemented
QED-CC methods in ExaChem, we examined proton transfer
between the cis and trans configurations of the porphyrin
molecule (162 electrons) with the cc-pVDZ (406 functions)
and cc-pVTZ (916 functions) basis sets. We conclude this
section with a brief discussion of the performance of the QED-
CC implementation based on calculations of the porphyrin
system.
In allcalculations, allelectrons andall orbitalsare correlated.
For the malonaldehyde and aminopropenal calculations, the
QEDparametersaresetto ωcav =3eVand λ=0.10au.Forthe
porphyrincalculations, theparameters are set to ωcav = 2.15 eV
and λ = 0.05 au. Please note that the coupling strengths used
here are larger than those typically realized in experiment.
These values are deliberately chosen to magnify QED-induced
effects and to maintain consistency with values used in prior
literature.65 Results for QED-HF and QED-CCSD are
reported as differences compared to their non-QED HF and
CCSD counterparts, whose values are also reported. We use
atomic units unless otherwise specified.
Lastly, the comparison of different basis sets for the three
proton transfer reactions serves to (1) demonstrate the
capabilities of the QED-CC methods in ExaChem and (2)
examine how large basis sets can change QED effects on
reactionbarriersandenergies.Protontransferreactionsarejust
one category of cavity-modified chemistry reactions that have
been studied. Further investigation beyond this work will be
needed to convincingly determine the role of larger basis sets
for other reactions and properties.
Malonaldehyde. The smallest system investigated in this
study was malonaldehyde, shown in Figure 2, which consists of
38 electrons. The reactant and product states in this proton
transfer reaction are identical; therefore, we report on the
changes in the reaction energy barrier (i.e., the energy
difference between the transition state and the reactant)
resulting from QED effects. Calculations were performed using
the cc-pVDZ basis set (90 functions) as well as the larger aug-
cc-pVDZ basis set (151 functions).
As summarized in Table 1, the non-QED HF method
overestimates the proton transfer reaction energy barrier by
approximately 4 kcal/mol for both basis sets when compared
to the canonical CCSD approach. For CCSD, we see that the
effect of the aug-cc-pVDZ basis set increases the reaction
barrier by 0.57 kcal/mol compared to the cc-pVDZ results.
Incorporating QED effects further modifies the reaction
energy barrier, with changes of −0.07 to 1.23 kcal/mol at the
QED-HF level and −0.24 to 1.14 kcal/mol at the QED-CC
level, depending on the basis set and the different photon
excitation levels. The largest QED effects are observed when
the polarization is aligned along the X direction, which
increases the reaction energy barrier by ∼1.1 kcal/mol for the
QED-CCSD(2,0) calculations. However, this effect is reduced
to ∼0.8 kcal/mol under QED-CCSD(2,2). Conversely,
polarization along the Z-axis decreases the reaction barrier
by 0.24 kcal/mol, which is reduced to 0.07 kcal/mol upon
switching to the aug-cc-pVDZ basis set. Notably, the impact of
basis set size on QED effects is minimal for the X and Y
polarizations, with changes of less than 0.02 kcal/mol when
transitioning from cc-pVDZ to aug-cc-pVDZ. The results
confirm that the cavity orientation significantly impacts the
reaction energy barrier. Under the ultrastrong coupling regime
used in these calculations, the influence of the QED effects is
similar in magnitude to the changes seen from expanding the
basis set size.

Performance. Having established in the previous section
that QED-CCSD(2,2) on porphyrin with cc-pVTZ is feasible
in ExaChem, the next question is scale: how large can we go
withcurrentresources?Toanswer,webenchmarksingleQED-
CCSD(2,2) iterations on porphyrin across node counts for cc-
pVDZ and cc-pVTZ.
All Porphyrin calculations presented in this paper were
performed on the Aurora supercomputer at the Argonne
Leadership Computing Facility (ALCF).90 Aurora is Intel’s
exascale system based on a heterogeneous architecture where
each compute node consists of two Intel Xeon Max Series
CPUs, code-named Sapphire Rapids, with integrated 128 GB
high-bandwidth memory (HBM2e) and 1024 GB DDR5
system memory. Each compute node also comprises six Intel
Data Center Max GPUs, codenamed Ponte Vecchio (PVC)
with 128 GB of HBM2e memory per GPU. Each PVC GPU is
further architecturally split into two Xe-stacks, often also
referred to as GPU tiles, with communication between them
handled via Intel’s Xe Link. System-wide communication is
facilitated by the Cray Slingshot-11 interconnect, which
employs a dragonfly topology to enable efficient and scalable
data transfer. All calculations on Aurora were performed using
12 MPI ranks per compute node, where each MPI rank was
bound to an Xe-Stack (GPU tile).
Table 4 details the per-iteration timings (as the average of
the first three iterations) for QED-CCSD(2,2) calculations at
various node counts. The timings reflect the computational
scaling behavior where the machine gives a healthy speed-up
before the gains flatten out. The cc-pVDZ and cc-pVTZ basis
sets exhibit favorable scaling trends up to 50 and 300 nodes,
respectively. These findings indicated that the calculations
could scale even further. As such, we compute the per-iteration
timings of the porphyrin systems with cc-pVQZ (1740 basis
functions) and def2-qzvpd (1974 basis functions). These
systems are roughly twice the size of cc-pVTZ at 917 basis
functions. Using 500 nodes, we can evaluate the residual
equations for cc-pVQZ and def2-qzvpd in 912 and 1186 s,
respectively. These results represent the largest QED-CCSD
calculations performed to date. The scaling analysis reinforces
the feasibility of tackling larger systems with this latest
implementation of the QED-CC methods in ExaChem.
■CONCLUSION
We have presented a high-performance, GPU-enabled
implementation of the QED-CCSD method within the
ExaChem program package. While the QED-CCSD formalism
itself is well established, its practical application to large-scale
systems has been limited by computational cost. By leveraging
the Tensor Algebra for Many-body Methods (TAMM)
infrastructure, automated equation generation with p†q, and
symbolic optimization via PQ-Graph, our implementation
delivers scalable performance across heterogeneous architec-
tures.
Benchmark calculations were performed on proton-transfer
reactions of the small molecules malonaldehyde and amino-
propenalaswellasthe162-electronporphyrinmolecule,where
QED effects altered the reaction energy by as much as 60%
relative to canonical CCSD. The magnitude of the energy
changes for the porphyrin system in comparison to the smaller
systems in this study indicates the necessity of accurate and
performant QED methods as system sizes grow. Timings of
calculations with up to 1974 basis functions demonstrate that
this new implementation can efficiently handle thousands of
basis functions and exploit hundreds of compute nodes on
leadership-class HPC systems.
