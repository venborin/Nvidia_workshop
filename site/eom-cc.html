From ground-state CC to excited states: The EOM idea

Ground-state CC gives a correlated reference |Ψ₀⟩ = eT̂|Φ₀⟩. The Equation-of-Motion (EOM) approach accesses excited, ionized, or electron-attached states by acting with a general excitation operator R̂ on this reference:

|Ψk⟩ = R̂k |Ψ₀⟩ = R̂k eT̂ |Φ₀⟩

The EOM eigenvalue problem uses the similarity-transformed Hamiltonian Ĥ̄ = e−T̂ Ĥ eT̂:

Ĥ̄ R̂k |Φ₀⟩ = Ek R̂k |Φ₀⟩

Because Ĥ̄ is non-Hermitian, left and right eigenvectors form a biorthogonal set. The key advantage: a single reference |Φ₀⟩ accesses an entire manifold of target states (EE, SF, IP, EA, DIP) by choosing different R̂ operators, avoiding the state-by-state orbital optimization required in multireference methods.

All EOM-CCSD variants scale as O(N⁶), same as CISD and ground-state CCSD (Krylov 2008, p. 4).

EOM-SF uses a high-spin triplet reference (MS=+1) to reach MS=0 singlet and triplet diradical states via a single spin-flip excitation. Why does this single-reference approach correctly describe the multiconfigurational diradical states, whereas a closed-shell reference with EOM-EE would require double excitations?

Accuracy benchmarks from Krylov (2008)

The review reports several quantitative accuracy measures for EOM-EE-CCSD:

  • Excitation energies: Conservative error bars 0.1–0.3 eV (p. 5); relative spacings reproduced more accurately.
  • Methylene (CH₂): ã¹A₁ – X³B₁ gap error 0.055 eV vs full CI; b̃¹B₁ – X³B₁ gap error 0.024 eV (p. 19).
  • Meta-xylylene diradical: Singlet-triplet gaps 0.49 eV (1¹A₁) and 1.19 eV (2¹A₁); closed-shell singlet ~0.5 eV above triplet (pp. 10–11).
  • 5-DMX triradical: Seven states within 3.5 eV; EOM-SF handles dense manifolds via balanced multistate treatment (p. 11).
  • Benzene dimer cation: Charge-resonance transition at 1.3 eV; EOM-IP properly describes charge localization where open-shell doublet CCSD fails (p. 14).

The EOM-EE-CCSD error bars (0.1–0.3 eV) are for absolute excitation energies, but relative state spacings are "usually reproduced more accurately." Why might error cancellation be more effective for energy differences than for absolute energies in EOM-CC?

Known limitations

The review identifies specific failure modes:

  • Doubly excited states: EOM-CCSD fails for states dominated by double excitations (methylene c̃¹A₁, Z-state in twisted ethylene, dark states in polyenes, IR states in NO dimer) because leading configurations appear at different excitation levels and are not treated in a balanced fashion (p. 19).
  • Spin contamination: Even with spin-pure (ROHF) references, target SF states show residual spin contamination because single and double excitations from an open-shell reference do not form a spin-complete set (p. 20).
  • Orbital relaxation in IP/EA/DIP: Error bars for EOM-IP/EA-CCSD are larger than for EOM-EE-CCSD; higher excitations (e.g., 3h2p for IP) seem necessary (p. 20). EOM-DIP straightforward implementation is unsatisfactory due to strong orbital-relaxation effects and physical instability of doubly attached references (p. 15).
  • Degenerate references: EOM-EE from a degenerate reference (e.g., Π state of OH) cannot reproduce Jahn-Teller degeneracy because components appear at different excitation levels (p. 19).
  • Extensive degeneracies: SF excitations fail for Be atom (2e in 3 orbitals) or O-He (4e in 4 orbitals) — not enough balanced determinants (p. 20).

EOM-CCSD includes doubly excited determinants in its wavefunction (via T₂ and R₂), yet fails for states where the leading configurations are doubly excited. Explain why the presence of double excitations in the ansatz is not sufficient — what does "balanced treatment" mean in this context?

Improvement directions

The review outlines several paths to higher accuracy and lower cost:

Accuracy improvements

  • Perturbative triples: Include triple excitations perturbatively rather than explicitly (refs 106–113); methods-of-moments approach (refs 114, 115).
  • Active-space triples: Restrict triples to a selected active space (refs 39, 116–119); energy-additivity schemes.
  • Similarity-transformed EOM-CC: Alternative formalism (refs 120, 121) showing promise for difficult cases.
  • Spin adaptation: Implement spin-adapted EOM-SF/EE for open-shell references (ref 65); especially important for higher-multiplicity references.
  • Fock-space detours: Combine multiple CC/EOM-CC schemes (e.g., for diradical/triradical stabilization energies, ref 75; for EAs in challenging systems, ref 57).

Computational cost reductions

  • Resolution of identity (RI) / density fitting: Reduces integral storage O(N⁴)→O(N³) and many contractions O(N⁶)→O(N⁵) (refs 122–124).
  • Reduced-scaling algorithms: Local correlation, pair natural orbitals (refs 125, 126); target near-linear scaling for large systems.
  • Parallelization: Distributed tensor contractions for production calculations.

Recent literature confirms progress on these fronts:

  • Projector-based embedding reduces EOM-CCSD cost for solvated systems to near-DFT scaling with <0.01 eV error (Bennie et al., J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 2017).
  • Periodic EOM-CCSD achieves 0.42 eV MAE for inorganic bandgaps; partitioned EOM-MP2 performs similarly at lower cost (Vo et al., J. Chem. Phys. 2024).
  • DLPNO-based IP-EOM-CCSD reaches >3000 basis functions with TightPNO accuracy ~0.1 eV vs canonical (Casanova-Páez & Neese, J. Chem. Theory Comput. 2026).

Perturbative triples (CCSD(T)-style) and active-space triples both avoid full O(N⁸) CCSDT. What is the fundamental difference in their approximations, and when would you choose one over the other for EOM-CC?

New applications enabled by EOM-CC

Beyond standard valence/Rydberg excitations, EOM-CC unlocks:

  • Diradicals & triradicals: EOM-SF from high-spin references gives balanced multistate description with error cancellation for singlet-triplet gaps (meta-xylylene, 5-DMX).
  • Bond breaking: σ-bond dissociation via ³σσ* reference; double-bond torsion via ³ππ* reference.
  • Charge-transfer systems: EOM-IP from closed-shell reference correctly describes charge localization in ionized dimers (benzene dimer cation, 10 low-lying states).
  • Conical intersections & JT systems: Multistate nature + simultaneous dynamical/nondynamical correlation captures state mixing and intersection topology (though non-Hermitian issues arise for same-symmetry intersections).
  • Sophisticated diradicals: EOM-DIP targets 4-electrons-in-3-orbitals systems (CH₂CH₂O, O-Rg complexes) at lower cost than EOM-SF — pending orbital-relaxation fixes.

EOM-IP from a closed-shell neutral reference correctly describes charge localization in the benzene dimer cation, whereas open-shell doublet CCSD overlocalizes. Why does changing the reference and excitation operator (IP vs EE) fix this problem? What property of the EOM-IP wavefunction ansatz makes it naturally suited for charge-transfer?
